1 · WCAG contrast — the numbers

Per W3C WCAG 2.1: normal text needs 4.5:1, large text needs 3:1 (SC 1.4.3), and meaningful graphics/UI need 3:1 against adjacent colors (SC 1.4.11). Ratios computed from the hex values. Note: W3C exempts text that is part of a logo or brand name from any contrast requirement — so the wordmark itself is compliant regardless, but meeting 3:1 is still best practice and is what the icon graphics should clear.

Read (updated palette): The brand blue was brightened from #2F6BFF to #3D82FF specifically to improve visibility — blue-on-navy went from a marginal 3.67:1 to a comfortable 4.59:1, so the icons now clear even the stricter text threshold, not just the graphics one. Trade-off: the brighter blue drops to 3.60:1 on white — still fine for the logo and large text, but below 4.5:1 for body copy, so use the deeper #1E5AF0 (below) for blue paragraph text on white. Two unchanged watch-outs: cyan on white is nearly invisible (1.62:1) — never use it for text/thin lines on white; and blue-beside-cyan is only 2.22:1, so those two colors must never be the only thing separating two elements — shape or spacing must carry it (as the chain-link's overlap does).

2 · Color-blindness simulation

The chain-link logo rendered as seen with the three main types of color vision deficiency (severe simulation, Machado et al. 2009). Red–green color blindness affects ~8% of males and ~0.5% of females of Northern European descent (US National Eye Institute).

Good news: the mark stays legible in all three. The two links read as separate because they overlap in shape, not because of their colors — so even when blue and cyan converge (protanopia/deuteranopia), you still see two interlocked links. This is exactly why it survives: it never depends on color alone. Blues are also relatively safe for the common red/green deficiencies: Colour Blindness Awareness notes that for people with both red and green deficiencies, "blues and yellows stand out." One genuine watch-out their guidance flags: red/green color-blind viewers can confuse blue with purple (they can't see the red component of purple) — our palette is pure blue/cyan with no purple, so we stay clear of that trap.

3 · What the color says (psychology)

Why blue "feels right" for this brand — and it's not just taste.

Blue is the most-preferred color. Survey research finds blue is the color both men and women are most likely to name as their favorite — not just in the US and Europe but in countries including China, Malaysia, and Indonesia (summarized in Eva Heller's Wie Farben wirken, cited by Wikipedia). Past US/European surveys most commonly associate blue with harmony, confidence, knowledge, intelligence, and calmness — a cluster that maps almost exactly onto what a security/infrastructure brand wants to project. That's why finance, enterprise software, and security products lean on it, and why it fits a protocol whose promise is "secure across the internet." (Color associations do vary by culture, so this is a strong tendency, not a universal law.)

Your specific mix maps neatly onto the brief:

  • Navy — depth, authority, engineering seriousness. Signals infrastructure you can rely on (think the AWS/enterprise register).
  • Electric Blue — energy and forward motion inside the trustworthy family. This is where "fast" lives without abandoning "safe."
  • Cyan — the modern, technical, high-tech spark. Reads as digital, current, and precise; keeps the mark from feeling corporate or dated.

The one caveat: blue is so common in tech that it can blur into the crowd. Your differentiation should therefore come from the cyan accent and the mark's shape, not the blue itself. The cyan is doing real brand work — keep it prominent as the signature, and you avoid the "generic tech blue" trap.

Recommendation

Keep the blue direction — it's the right call. It's accessible where it matters (on navy), it's the most color-blind-safe hue family, and it psychologically signals exactly the trust + security your protocol sells. Three small guardrails will make it bulletproof:
  • Treat cyan as accent-only, never text on white. Cyan on white is 1.62:1 — illegible. Use cyan on navy (10:1, excellent) or as a fill, not as small type on light backgrounds.
  • Never rely on blue-vs-cyan alone to carry meaning. They're only 2.78:1 apart. In the logo you're fine because shape separates the links — just keep that principle in any UI (add labels/icons, don't color-code by these two alone).
  • Optional: a "text blue" for light backgrounds. If you want blue body text or links on white to clear AA comfortably, a slightly deeper blue (e.g. Aa #1E5AF0 = 5.56:1) reads as the same brand blue but passes with margin. Keep #3D82FF as the logo/brand blue; use the deeper one only for small text on white.
